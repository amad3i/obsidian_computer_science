# Principle of good enough

Principle of good enough (often referred to as the "good enough" principle) is a heuristic and product philosophy in software engineering, systems design, and product management. It posits that a system or product that fulfills minimal core requirements, and is therefore "good enough" for the immediate needs of the user, is often preferable to delaying release in favor of a theoretically perfect or feature-complete alternative.
The principle advocates for pragmatism, speed, and resource efficiency. It relies on the assumption that users prefer early access to a functional solution over a delayed, perfect one. However, the concept is a subject of debate within the software industry. Critics, such as Pete McBreen, warn that the approach can serve as a justification for lower quality standards. This can potentially lead to technical debt and long-term maintenance issues.
In the context of the good enough principle, this theory provides the justification for halting development once core requirements are met. Continuing to search for a "perfect" solution (optimization) yields diminishing returns and incurs opportunity costs that outweigh the marginal benefits of further improvement.
